Should You Scope the Sewer Line Before Buying a Mission Viejo Home?

The sewer line is the one big plumbing item a standard home inspection usually skips. Here is why it is worth a look.

By the Mission Viejo Pro Plumbing team · Published October 2, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • A sewer scope runs a camera through the lateral from the house cleanout to the connection with the street main.
  • Standard home inspections usually do not include it, so it has to be ordered separately.
  • Mission Viejo's first homes date to 1966, so many laterals are now more than 50 years old.
  • Book the scope early in the inspection period, so findings can inform your decision or your negotiation.
The Gap

What a Normal Home Inspection Leaves Out

A general home inspector looks at what can be seen and tested without tools that go underground. That means the inspector flushes toilets and runs fixtures to check drainage, but does not push a camera down the buried line. Many standard inspection reports state that the sewer lateral is outside their scope.

That gap matters because the lateral is expensive to replace and impossible to check by eye. A line can drain fine on inspection day and still have cracked joints, a belly that holds water or roots that have found the joints. The first sign is often a backup after you move in.

Mission Viejo's first families moved into homes sold in 1966, and Casta del Sol's 1,927 homes were built between 1972 and 1987. A lateral from those years has been in the ground for four to six decades.

What the Camera Finds

Timing

When to Book the Scope

California purchase contracts commonly give the buyer an inspection period of about 17 days, though terms vary with the deal. Book the sewer scope in the first few days, because a defect found late leaves little time to negotiate a repair credit, ask for a seller repair or cancel.

Ask the listing agent for access to the cleanout, usually a capped pipe outside near the house. If there is no cleanout, the camera can enter through a toilet or roof vent, and the plumber may recommend adding one. Ask to be present, or ask for the full recording.

What to Get From the Inspection

  • A video recording of the whole lateral, with distance markings.
  • A written summary of each defect, with its distance from the cleanout and a plain-language grade.
  • A locator mark on the ground showing the path and depth of the pipe.
  • A statement of whether the camera reached the connection to the street main.
  • A price range for any repair the plumber recommends, so you can use it in negotiation.
Reading the Report

How to Use the Findings

A thorough report grades each defect with a standard method such as the NASSCO Pipeline Assessment Certification Program coding, which rates cracks, roots and breaks consistently. Ask the plumber which method the report uses, because a grade is easier to defend in a negotiation than an opinion.

In California the district generally owns the main in the street and the homeowner owns the lateral to the house, though each district sets its own boundary. The footage shows which side any damage sits on, and that decides who pays. Santa Margarita, Moulton Niguel and El Toro Water Districts serve parts of Mission Viejo.

If the scope shows serious damage, a buyer can ask the seller to repair it, request a credit, or walk away within the contingency period. Minor findings, such as light scale or small roots, are usually a maintenance note instead of a deal point. For the inspection itself, see our sewer camera inspection page.

Beyond Mission Viejo

Older Homes in Nearby Cities

The same logic holds in older neighborhoods nearby. San Juan Capistrano has homes that range from the 1970s back to the early 1900s, and a scope there can find clay or mixed-material pipe with joints that have moved.

Whether a city requires a lateral inspection at sale is a local matter, so ask your agent or the city. A required inspection does not replace a scope done for your own protection.

Cost and Value

Why a Scope Is Small Money Next to What It Can Save

A camera inspection costs a small fraction of a sewer line replacement, which is one of the larger plumbing projects a homeowner can face. The scope turns an unknown into a known number before you commit to the purchase. Even a clean result has value, because it tells you the line was sound on the day you bought.

If the scope turns up a problem, the plumber can price the repair while you are still inside the contingency window. That price is your negotiating anchor, and a written quote from a plumber carries more weight than a general sense that the pipe is old.

For Sellers

If You Are Selling: Why a Scope Helps You Too

A seller who scopes the line first controls the story. A clean report reassures buyers and shortens negotiations. A report with a modest defect lets the seller fix it quietly or disclose it with a quote attached, instead of discovering it under pressure in the middle of escrow.

California disclosure rules ask sellers to reveal known defects, including known problems with plumbing and sewer systems. If a scope shows damage, treat it as known, and talk to your agent about disclosure before the listing goes live.
