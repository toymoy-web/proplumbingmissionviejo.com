Slab Leaks

Slab Leak Detection and Repair in Mission Viejo

A slab leak is a leak in a water supply line that runs beneath or inside the concrete foundation of a house. The pipe is out of sight, so the first sign is usually a symptom somewhere else: a spinning meter, a warm patch on the floor, or the sound of water in a quiet room.

Casta del Sol added 1,927 mostly single-story homes between 1972 and 1987, and single-story houses on concrete slabs are where these leaks show up. Copper lines laid in that era have decades of wear, and a pinhole in a hot water line can run for weeks before a bill or a damp carpet gives it away.

Why It Happens

Why Slab Leaks Show Up in Mission Viejo Homes

Many Mission Viejo homes built from the late 1960s through the 1980s sit on concrete slabs with copper supply lines running through or under them. Over decades copper can corrode from the outside where it touches concrete or soil, and from the inside through pitting. Water hammer and high street pressure add wear, and a pinhole is small but constant under pressure.

Symptoms

Signs You May Have a Slab Leak

A warm spot on the floor

A hot water line leaking under the slab can heat the concrete above it, and bare feet notice before anything else.

The meter keeps moving

With every fixture off, a meter that still turns points to a leak on the supply side.

Sound of running water

A hiss or trickle with no fixture running suggests pressurized water is escaping.

Damp flooring or baseboards

Lifting carpet, cupped wood floors or wet drywall at the bottom of a wall can trace back to the slab.

What to Do Right Away

  • Close the main water shutoff and see whether the sound or the meter movement stops.
  • Turn off the water heater if the leak is on the hot side, so it does not run dry and overheat.
  • Lift rugs and move furniture off wet flooring, and photograph the damage for your insurer.
  • Do not cut into the floor yourself, since the wet spot is often not above the leak.

Repair Options

Three Ways to Fix a Leak Under the Floor

Spot repair breaks a small opening in the slab above the leak, replaces the damaged section and patches the concrete. It is the least disruptive option when the pipe is otherwise sound and the leak is isolated.

Rerouting abandons the leaking line and runs a new one through the attic or walls to the same fixtures. It avoids concrete work and suits homes where the line has failed more than once.

Repiping the home replaces the supply system with new pipe. Our repiping page explains when it makes sense, which is usually after a second leak in aging copper. Any new or rerouted water piping must meet the California Plumbing Code, built on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and be pressure tested before it is covered.

After the Trace

What Happens Once the Leak Is Located

Before any work starts, the plumber explains where the leak is and how deep it sits. Three paths are possible, a spot repair through the floor, a reroute through the attic or walls, or a full repipe. The water to that line is usually shut off while the choice is made, and a hot-water line leak can mean no hot water until the repair is finished.

Rerouting and repiping normally require a permit and an inspection. Ask who pulls it and what the quote includes for patching drywall and flooring, since those are the costs that surprise people.

Your Floors

Protecting Floors and Belongings During the Repair

Tile and hardwood need a plan. Ask whether the floor will be opened by cutting a small square or a long channel, how matching tile will be found and how dust will be contained. Furniture and rugs come off the repair area before work begins.

If water has reached the floor covering, drying matters as much as the pipe. A trace comes first through leak detection, and water damage restoration handles the drying if water spread.

The Clues

Warm Spots, Late Hot Water and the Water Heater Clue

A leak on the hot line leaves a warm spot on the floor, and the water heater can show it too. A heater that cycles on and off more often than usual, or hot water that takes much longer to arrive, may be feeding a leak underground. The heater is fine in that case and the loss is in the pipe.

Cold-line leaks are quieter. The usual signs are a meter that moves with every fixture off, a damp patch on the slab edge outside, or a hissing sound at night. Tell the plumber which line you suspect so the first test is aimed at the right pipe.
